# Veteran Techniques — curated from Burn.Life [evergreen unless tagged]

This section summarizes field-tested build and living techniques from Burn.Life, a
non-commercial hobbyist Burning Man site written by veteran burner Dr. Yes. The
knowledge below is restated in Playa Pal's own words with per-topic credit; the full
guides live at burn.life and are worth reading before the burn. Where a Burn.Life
number differs from the official 2026 Survival Guide, the official number wins (see
the Water and Hydration section: the ration is 1.5 gallons per person per day).
Prices and vendor details from the source era are tagged [era: 2014-2016 — verify].

## Anchoring: lag screws instead of rebar [evergreen technique]
- Veteran alternative to rebar for staking tents, shade structures, and tarps: hex-head
  lag screws driven straight into the playa with a cordless drill (impact driver best,
  regular drill fine) and a 3/8" socket bit. No pilot hole — tight packing grips.
- Sizing: 3/8" hex-head lag screws, sold in 10", 12", 14". 12" held a large shade
  structure through storms. Monkey-hut ribs are the exception — they still want ~24"
  rebar (the PVC rib slides over 8-10" of exposed rebar).
- Anchor point trick: before driving, thread the screw through one link of a 2-link
  chain segment. Use chain thick enough that the screw passes but the hex head does
  not — the head pins link 1 flush to the ground and link 2 becomes your anchor loop
  (no washers needed). Buy 3 chain links per screw; cut every third link off-playa.
- Why over rebar: sits flush (no shin-spearing trip hazard), needs no guy-lines of its
  own, goes in and out in ~5 seconds, reusable for years, similar cost.
- Leaving: unscrew, bag, done. Charge drill batteries fully; bring spares.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Lag Screws 101](https://www.burn.life/lag-screws-101.html) (Dr. Yes, curating a guide by u/ApesInSpace; technique by FIGJAM). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Anchoring: rebar, if you must [evergreen technique]
- Where rebar still makes sense (monkey-hut ribs, or you already own it): use 18" or
  24" lengths in the #4 size (about 1/2" thick), driven fully in.
- Drive it with a metal-head mallet — plastic mallets get chewed apart by rebar.
- Cover every exposed end: cut a slit in a tennis ball and push it on. Exposed rebar
  ends have opened real ankles; a fall onto bare rebar can injure badly.
- Removal trick: clamp locking vice-grip pliers a few inches above the base, then
  lever against a piece of 2x4 to walk the bar out of the ground.
- The stakes bundled with a cheap tent will bend and pull out in a real playa blow.
  If weak stakes are all you have, keep something dense inside the tent (a loaded
  cooler works) so a collapsed tent at least stays on the ground instead of flying.
- A tent loose in a windstorm is a hazard to the whole neighborhood, not just to you.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Tents and Shade](https://www.burn.life/tents-and-shade.html) and [Lag Screws 101](https://www.burn.life/lag-screws-101.html) (Dr. Yes; lag-screw technique by FIGJAM). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Shade: Aluminet, shade cloth, carports [evergreen technique]
- Effectiveness ladder: full carport/canopy frame > woven shade cloth > Aluminet;
  portability and setup ease run the other way.
- Aluminet (woven reflective mesh) over the tent is the easy big win: it reflects
  solar load instead of absorbing it. It comes in strengths — 70% is the strongest
  commonly sold and worth it. Result reported: sleeping until 10:30-11am inside a tent
  that would otherwise be an oven by 8:30.
- A carport-style canopy (the common 10'x20' class) over the whole tent is the gold
  standard — light steel frame, heavy fabric ceiling, optional walls — but bulky, and
  it MUST be staked like you mean it (see Anchoring). Playa windstorms destroy
  casually-staked canopies.
- A monkey hut (PVC-rib quonset over rebar) is as good as a carport and cheaper if
  you are willing to build it.
- Whatever the shade, the tent under it still needs a strong metal frame and serious
  stakes or lag screws — bundled big-box stakes fail exactly when the storm hits.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Tents and Shade](https://www.burn.life/tents-and-shade.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Shade: canopy wind safety and cooling the tent [evergreen technique]
- Canopy walls act as sails: side panels that do not pass wind can turn the whole
  structure into a kite. Guy-wire canopies aggressively; the winds reach gale force.
- The cheap 10'x10' pop-up shades are cheap for a reason. If one comes anyway, anchor
  it with multiple guy lines and deeply driven stakes or lag screws, never its own
  feet alone.
- Cooling options inside a tent, in rising order of effort: battery or plug-in fans;
  a swamp cooler; an air conditioner if your camp grid or generator can power it
  (vent it properly).
- Swamp coolers love the playa: they need exactly what the desert has — bone-dry air —
  plus air circulation. The classic DIY design is FIGJAM's bucket cooler. Reported
  field result: it could not chill a whole Shiftpod's airspace, but ducting its cool
  air under the bed sheets was almost too cold. Aim the cold at the sleeper, not the
  room.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Tents and Shade](https://www.burn.life/tents-and-shade.html) (Dr. Yes; bucket-cooler design by FIGJAM). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Shelter: choosing a playa tent [evergreen technique]
- What matters, in order: sturdiness (strong metal frame + serious anchoring — winds
  reach gale force), dust-sealing, waterproofing (real rainstorms do happen), interior
  height you can stand in, and zippers strong enough to yank when dust cakes them.
- Dust-sealing test: every mesh opening needs a solid fabric flap that zips over it.
  Mesh-only panels mean everything inside wears a visible dust layer after one storm.
  Mesh can be retrofitted: sew or tape solid fabric over it.
- Canvas tents win on the playa if the budget allows: heavier, far more dust- and
  wind-resistant, genuinely rain-proof, and they last decades with care.
- Two proven picks from years of use: the Kodiak 10'x14' canvas cabin tent (stand-up
  height, excellent build, overhead storage, near dust-proof sealed up) and the
  Shiftpod 2 (roughly twice the Kodiak's price; sets up and tears down in minutes,
  roomier vertical walls, built-in ports for AC or swamp-cooler ducting).
- The Shiftpod's reflective skin still wants real shade over it; treat "reflective"
  as a bonus, not a shade plan.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Tents and Shade](https://www.burn.life/tents-and-shade.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Shelter: sleeping clean and organized [evergreen technique]
- Elevate the bed: a camping bed frame keeps the mattress off the floor, which keeps
  the daily dust film off your bedding, opens bin storage underneath, and simply
  feels more like a real bed at 4am.
- Cover the sleeping surface with a plain sheet during the day; whatever dust sneaks
  in lands on the sheet, not the pillow. Shake it out at night.
- Shoes stay outside the door — wearing them in is the fastest way to coat the tent.
- Live out of tubs or plastic drawer sets; a tent you can find things in feels twice
  as big by Thursday.
- Keep the constantly-grabbed items (sunscreen, sunglasses, goggles) in a pocket
  right at the tent entrance so nobody tracks dust in to fetch them.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Tents and Shade](https://www.burn.life/tents-and-shade.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Coolers: making ice last the week [evergreen technique]
- Freeze large 2.5-liter square-ish drinking-water bottles at home and load them in as
  your ice. Reported result: still ice a week later — and the melt is drinking water,
  not a soggy food bath.
- Pre-cool every beverage before it goes in; warm cans burn ice fast.
- Open the cooler as rarely as possible. Keep it in shade, always.
- Elevate it off the playa on a couple of pieces of wood — ground contact is a big
  heat leak.
- Cheap cooler upgrade: tape reflective insulation tightly around the whole body
  (double layer, smallest possible gap at the lid), or just wrap it in a quilt.
- Premium coolers (Yeti/Engel/Pelican class) genuinely perform: a 65-qt example ran
  Saturday load-out to Thursday on playa before needing Arctica ice.
- Pace the menu around the cooler: eat fresh food early in the week, frozen food late.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Food and Water](https://www.burn.life/food-and-water.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Menu: eating when it is too hot to be hungry [evergreen technique]
- Appetite shrinks on playa. A realistic pattern: one real meal a day plus nutritious
  snacks, and lots of water. Eat anyway — the body still needs fuel.
- Salt matters: you sweat it out all day, so salty food is both needed and tastes
  right out there.
- Boil-in-bag is the tent-camp power move: cook stews, curries, or meatballs at home,
  seal them in bags, then boil the bag on a small propane stove and eat from it — no
  dishes. Freeze-dried camping meals (Mountain House class) work the same way.
- Instant oatmeal owns breakfast: boiling water is the whole recipe.
- A big pasta salad with meat or tofu keeps all week in the cooler and covers lunch.
- Soup works — and if the stove feels like too much, cold soup is honestly fine there.
- Squeeze pouches of apple sauce or quality baby food, kept cold, go down easily when
  nothing else sounds appealing. Nuts, hard cheese, and nut butter carry the between
  hours. Fresh fruit and vegetables feel like luxury by mid-week — bring some.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Food and Water](https://www.burn.life/food-and-water.html) and [Burning on a Budget](https://www.burn.life/burning-on-a-budget.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Bikes: playa-proofing and not losing it [evergreen technique]
- The right playa bike is cheap, comfortable (beach-cruiser posture), and wears fat
  tires for soft playa. Single-speed is fine — the city is flat, and gears are just
  more parts for dust to kill. Playa dust is corrosive; most burners keep a dedicated
  playa bike and simply store it dirty between years.
- Tennis-ball trick: slice a hole in a tennis ball and push it onto the kickstand
  foot. Otherwise soft playa swallows the kickstand and the bike falls over.
- Write your name, camp, and playa address on a slip of paper and seal it to the
  frame with clear packing tape — the honest way lost or "enthusiastically borrowed"
  bikes come home the next morning.
- Decorate distinctively (fur, fabric, zip ties) — it is self-expression AND the only
  way to find yours in a rack of hundreds. Lights at night: to be seen, and to find it.
- Add a front or rear basket if you will be hauling ice from Arctica.
- Lock it, but NEVER to art unless the piece is explicitly marked for bike parking.
  At big burns and sound camps, lock up a short walk away — the crowd grows and
  swallows whatever is parked at the center. Steer around soft dust piles.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Bikes](https://www.burn.life/bikes.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Clothing: playa foot and the always-carry three [evergreen technique]
- Feet first: constant exposure to the alkaline dust cracks skin until it can bleed —
  the dreaded playa foot. Closed shoes or boots with socks, day and night, prevent
  it. Choose comfort over costume for footwear; you will walk miles.
- Pack two pairs of socks and underwear for every day. Fresh socks when everything
  else is dusty is one of the event's small miracles.
- Never leave camp without the three: goggles that seal around the eyes (whiteouts
  arrive fast), a dust mask (a bandana over the mouth genuinely works), and water —
  a hands-free carrier like a hydration pack beats any bottle, and lights on the
  pack make you visible from behind.
- Nights get cold — occasionally near freezing, and almost always chilly by 3am.
  Layer up BEFORE going out for the night and bring the warm coat you doubt you need.
- Be lit after dark, front and back — glow sticks or battery EL wire on your person.
  An unlit walker (a "darkwad") is the classic night hazard for bikes and art cars.
- Playa clothes never fully recover; assume whatever comes to the burn stays burner
  wardrobe. No need to overbuy: a basic base layer plus bold accessories reads as a
  full costume.

## Entry: the drive in [technique + tactics]
- Fuel discipline: top up in Gerlach (or Nixon on the way up) no matter what the
  gauge says. You want maximum range in the tank against a slow exodus at week's end.
- From the moment you leave I-80, drive exactly the limit — not one mph over. Local,
  county, and state patrols work that corridor hard, and a stop can become a search
  (K-9 units included). Do not pull onto soft shoulders; vehicles bog down instantly.
- Bike racks: if bikes hide the license plate, zip-tie the plate to the rearmost bike
  and light it after dark — an obscured plate is a classic pretext for a pull-over.
- Keep a full day of drinking water reachable in the cabin, even if the week's supply
  rides with someone else. Gate waits of 6+ hours happen at peak (roughly mid-day
  Sunday through Tuesday night), and weather closures have stretched waits far longer.
- No partying in the entry line — DUI law applies and is enforced there.
- Timing: arrive as the gates open, or from Wednesday on, for the shortest lines; and
  land with enough daylight left to build camp.
- Inside the city: 5 mph, watch for the unlit and the unsteady, park at camp, and the
  car stays parked until exodus.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Getting to Burning Man (Entry)](https://www.burn.life/getting-to-burning-man-entry.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Exodus: leaving strategy [technique + tactics]
- The whole pain is one two-lane road. Departures from Sunday mid-morning through
  Monday risk the long queue — anywhere from 45 minutes to 8 hours, and unpredictable.
- The veteran windows: leave Saturday evening before the Man burns, or very early
  Sunday morning after it — both typically see minimal line.
- De-MOOP before you roll: sweep your footprint and take everything that is not playa
  dust. Garbage can be dropped in Gerlach for a few dollars a bag
  [era: 2014-2016 — verify price].
- Tune BMIR 94.5 FM for live wait estimates before committing to the line. If the
  estimate is ugly, an extra evening at camp with friends beats an evening in the
  queue.
- Pulsing: lanes stand fully stopped (engines off, stretch, meet the neighbors) for
  stretches, then release all at once — expect surges, not creep.
- Book the post-burn Reno hotel in advance; it is a holiday weekend and rooms sell
  out. The first shower and mediocre diner meal will feel like heaven.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Leaving Burning Man (Exodus)](https://www.burn.life/leaving-burning-man-exodus.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## RVs: power, capacity, and dust operations [technique]
- Generator sizing is about STARTING the air conditioner, not running it: start draw
  exceeds the advertised running draw, so you typically need at least 3,000 watts to
  kick an RV AC over. Choose an inverter generator — the quiet kind — over the loud
  contractor units rental companies push.
- "Sleeps 6" is a legal fiction of fold-out benches. Two people in a rental RV is
  comfortable; every body beyond that costs real livability and all privacy.
- Dust discipline: door closed always, shoes off at the step, clean up after storms
  before entering — and keep the ceiling vents SHUT unless you want a dune inside
  after the first blow.
- Black water is finite: many crews run a #1-only toilet rule to stretch it. Pump-out
  trucks do roam the city, but mid-week service is hit-or-miss — reserve with the
  service months ahead if the tank must last.
- Book early: California and Nevada rental fleets sell out around February. Delivered-
  to-playa RV packages start around $4,000 for the week [era: 2014-2016 — verify].
  Rent from a company that knows where the RV is going — the dust is unhideable.
- Budget packing-out time: an RV un-camps far slower than a tent.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — RVs](https://www.burn.life/rvs.html) and [Poopin' on the Playa](https://www.burn.life/poopin-on-the-playa.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Sanitation: beyond the porta-potty line [technique]
- The cold-3am answer is a dedicated pee jug in the tent: use it at night, pour it
  into a porta-potty later. Pour the CONTENTS only — never toss the jug itself in.
- Women: carry a pee funnel. Pee Funnel Camp gifts them free on playa, and camping
  stores sell reusable ones (She-Wee class). Veterans call this a week-changer.
- A real bucket toilet is legitimate: a sealable bucket with cat litter or dry soil
  absorbs and controls a #2. It leaves the playa WITH you for legal disposal at home —
  never buried, never in Gerlach bins, never in a regular dumpster.
- Camps can rent a private porta-potty from companies near Reno/Sparks
  [era: 2014-2016 — verify vendors], but only if the vendor can service it on playa;
  an unserviced private potty becomes an unusable biohazard by mid-week.
- Carry your own toilet paper and hand sanitizer — the public portas run out of both.
- Wipes and tampons never go down the hole: bag them (a clean-bag/dirty-bag pair
  inside one carrier bag works) and pack them out.
- Sit; do not hover. Close the lid. Everyone after you benefits.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Poopin' on the Playa](https://www.burn.life/poopin-on-the-playa.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Cameras: dust discipline for photos [evergreen technique]
- Default state: camera sealed in a plastic bag, always, whenever it is not actively
  shooting. Never bring it out during a dust storm.
- Small sealed compact beats big glass on accessibility: a pocketable camera in a
  zip-bag in a hip pouch gets used constantly; a DSLR buried in a bagged backpack
  does not.
- Consider renting (with insurance) instead of risking an expensive unsealed body —
  post-burn cleanup of a rental is alcohol swabs and a return label.
- Telescoping zoom lenses pump dusty air into the body with every extend/retract
  cycle. Prefer internal-zoom or prime lenses, or baby the telescoper.
- Changing lenses: do it inside the tent or RV, inside a garbage bag nested in a
  second garbage bag, hands working within the bag. Dust on the sensor is forever
  (until a professional cleaning).
- Night shots need high-ISO performance, a fast lens, and usually a tripod; without
  those, shoot video instead or just put the camera down.
- People etiquette: ask before photographing anyone, and never publish shots of
  partially-dressed participants without explicit permission. Commercial or
  non-personal projects must follow the official Media Rights process.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Cameras](https://www.burn.life/cameras.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Leave No Trace: a camp trash system that works [technique]
- Four containers, sorted at the moment of tossing: (1) burnables — paper, food
  scraps; (2) glass and plastic recyclables; (3) aluminum cans; (4) non-burnable
  trash. Color-code the bins and label them; collapsible laundry baskets pack flat.
- Unwrap everything at home before packing — packaging you never bring is MOOP you
  never manage.
- During the week: burnables go to burn barrels or the camp burn pit nightly;
  aluminum goes to Recycle Camp (the one recycling operation in the city); glass and
  plastic ride home — Reno redemption centers even pay for them.
- Done right, the drive home carries almost nothing but a light bag of true trash.
- Departure sweep: first each person combs their own site down to splinter and
  glitter scale, then the whole camp walks the footprint shoulder-to-shoulder in a
  line — twice. The cleanup volunteers who inspect afterward map every camp's MOOP
  (green/yellow/red), and the event's federal land permit literally depends on
  passing.
- Greywater — shower, dish, or wash water — never touches the playa.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Leave No Trace](https://www.burn.life/leave-no-trace.html) (Dr. Yes, reprinting a system by burner ShiftyFox). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Adventuring: the going-out kit [evergreen technique]
- The rule: go more than a street or two from camp and goggles, dust mask, and water
  come with you — no exceptions. Whiteouts do not schedule themselves.
- After dark add lighting visible from front AND back before leaving camp at all.
- Carry a reusable cup: camps and strangers offer drinks constantly, and nobody
  pours into cupped hands. Bars really do check ID — a photocopy taped to the cup
  means the real one stays safe at camp [note: some bars insist on the physical ID].
- Toilet paper and hand sanitizer live permanently in the day pack; the porta you
  need will be the one that ran out.
- Sun protection before leaving, every time. A playa burn is a week-ruiner.
- Navigate by landmarks that will not be burned down mid-week; the city is easy to
  read once you learn its clock-and-ring logic and very disorienting before that.
- In trouble? Find a Black Rock Ranger — the volunteers in khaki. They are not police
  and exist to help participants.
- Stopped art cars can be asked for a ride; that is what they are for.

*Credit: [Burn.Life — Adventuring on the Playa](https://www.burn.life/adventuring-on-the-playa.html) (Dr. Yes). Retrieved 2026-08-14.*

## Budget: where the money actually goes [technique]
- Beyond ticket and transport, a modest-comfort tent week costs roughly $100-500 more
  depending on what can be borrowed [era: 2014-2016 — verify].
- The never-skip list, even at maximum cheapness: dust mask (a bandana counts),
  sealing goggles, night lights for your body, a way to carry water, and real warm
  clothing for the cold nights. These are safety, not comfort.
- Water is not a savings line: 1.5 gallons per person per day, extra if showering.
- A borrowed or secondhand tent is fine — spend the savings on anchoring (18"+
  stakes, rebar, or lag screws and rope). An Aluminet sheet is the budget shade that
  buys sleep past sunrise; camping under a friend's shade structure is even cheaper.
- The budget kitchen: small propane stove + one pot, boil-in-bag dinners cooked at
  home, instant oatmeal breakfasts, a week-long pasta salad for lunches.
- Free costumes exist on playa: outfitting camps (listed in the event guide) dress
  people for nothing, and thrift stores handle the rest — loud colors fit right in.
- Radical self-reliance is the budget's floor: arriving planning to live off other
  camps' food and water makes you a burden, not a burner. And never try to sneak in —
  vehicles are searched, the perimeter is watched at night, and a caught stowaway
  gets the entire vehicle's tickets voided.
